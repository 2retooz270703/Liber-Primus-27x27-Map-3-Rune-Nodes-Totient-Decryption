## The First Book of Urizen

*The First Book of Urizen* was created by William Blake in 1794. Urizen is a figure from Blake’s own mythology, associated with reason, law, limitation, separation from eternity, and the attempt to confine reality within rigid boundaries.

The book is not a travel narrative in the ordinary sense. It is a myth about a consciousness separating itself from the infinite, creating a limited world of its own, becoming trapped inside that world, and then wandering through and examining the reality it has created.

Blake presents the work almost like an alternative Book of Genesis: a personal myth of creation and the fall of humanity. Its chapters and verses deliberately resemble biblical structure, while the strict and highly ordered form of the text reflects one of the book’s central ideas — Urizen’s attempt to impose measure, law, and structure on an infinite and chaotic reality.

The word “First” in the title does not mean that Blake later wrote a numbered second or third *Book of Urizen*. No such sequence exists. In fact, modern editions sometimes title the work *The [First] Book of Urizen* because the word “First” does not appear consistently across all surviving copies and editions.

---

## What Happens in *The First Book of Urizen*

At the beginning, there is something like an eternal, undivided state. Then Urizen emerges as a separate force. He becomes self-enclosed, cut off from the other Eternals and confined within a space of his own. His realm is associated with the North, darkness, shadow, emptiness, and solitude. Urizen wants to put an end to uncertainty. He wants there to be one law, one measure, one correct system. Before this separation, reality is infinite, fluid, and cannot be reduced to a single structure. Urizen, in effect, declares:

> “I will define the boundaries.  
> I will establish the laws.  
> I will measure the world.  
> I will decide what is possible and what is not.”

By doing this, he begins to create a limited reality.

---

## Urizen Measuring the Abyss

And this is where things become especially interesting in relation to the geometry. Urizen literally begins to measure the Abyss. This is not just a modern interpretation — in the text itself, he creates instruments of measurement. Blake lists a line, a plummet, a dividing rule, scales and weights, a quadrant, and finally a golden compass. With these tools, Urizen begins to explore the Abyss. His philosophical error is not simply that he is “evil,” but that he tries to reduce the infinite to something measurable, ordered, and confined within a system.

He takes the Abyss and imposes upon it:

> **measure → division → geometry → laws → boundaries**

In other words, he attempts to turn infinity into a mathematical structure.

---

## Urizen’s Journey

Later, Urizen finds himself inside the very world he has created, and this is where the story begins to resemble an actual journey. In Chapter VIII, he explores his caves and domains — mountains, wastelands, and wilderness — carrying a fiery globe to light his way. Blake explicitly calls it:

> “A fearful journey”

This matters because Urizen is no longer simply a creator looking down on his creation from outside. He is now moving through it from within, confronting the consequences of the system he built. The world is filled with distorted and fragmented forms of life, his own sons and daughters suffer, and Urizen discovers something devastating: life does not obey his iron laws. He has created a perfect system, but living beings cannot fit within it.

And then the cold appears. This is especially striking in relation to my hypothetical plaintext:

> **AS I GO, THE WEATHER TURNS COLD. I MAY CRY NOW.**

After seeing what his world has become, Urizen weeps.

---

## The Cold Shadow and the Net

Then Blake writes:

> “Cold he wander’d…”

Urizen wanders over the cities in weeping, pain, and sorrow, while a cold shadow follows him. Blake describes this shadow like a web — damp, cold, and dim — almost as if it is being drawn out from Urizen’s own sorrowing soul. This is a striking moment because the world around him begins to feel like a projection of his inner state. It is not simply that Urizen feels cold; rather, his inner coldness becomes part of the structure of the world itself.

And that structure eventually becomes a net.

Blake describes this dark, cold web spreading everywhere, tangled and interconnected, with its knots compared to the structure of the human brain. In the end, he gives it a name:

> **The Net of Religion**

In other words, law, dogma, fear, and limited perception form a literal network of restriction — a structure from which the inhabitants can no longer escape.

---

## The Labyrinth as a Prison of Perception

Another important shift follows: human perception becomes narrower. People can no longer perceive infinity and begin to mistake their limited world for the whole of reality. This is one of Blake’s central ideas:

> The prison does not exist only around a person.  
> The prison is built into the way a person perceives the world.

Eventually, people no longer even realize that they are living inside a system. In that sense, the book is less about Urizen simply going somewhere and more about Urizen creating a labyrinth and then becoming trapped within it himself.

And the ending is not a conventional story of escape.

> **He does not find a way out.**

The fallen world remains.

---

## What Blake Was Trying to Say

The deepest idea, in my view, is not simply that “logic is bad.” That would be far too simplistic. Blake is criticizing a form of reason that mistakes itself for the whole of reality. Urizen is the kind of reason that says:

> If I cannot measure it, define it, or contain it within a law, then it does not exist.

This is why Urizen is often associated with “your reason,” even though the origin of the name is more complicated. In Blake’s mythology, Urizen becomes the embodiment of rational order, law, limitation, and restriction.

The opposite of this is not nonsense, but **Imagination**: the ability to see the whole, to perceive connections, infinity, and possibilities beyond an established system.

That creates a striking paradox:

> **Reason creates a map of the world → forgets that the map is not the world itself → becomes trapped inside its own map.**

---

## Reason, Imagination, and the Labyrinth

A rigid rule can reveal only one part of a system, while Imagination allows you to see the relationships between those parts and the larger structure they form. In the context of the labyrinth, this would not mean abandoning formulas. It would mean recognizing their limits:

> **formulas provide local constraints → geometry reveals recurring structures → but the correct path emerges when you notice a higher-level pattern that cannot be reduced to a single mechanical operation**

That is very close to the way I approached the plaintext. I did not simply choose a word because it sounded right. I found several possible branches, then noticed that one of them returned to familiar geometry, the same kinds of nodes, totient relationships, mirror structures, and other recurring features.

That is not arbitrary imagination. It is closer to **pattern recognition**: seeing a structure that is not explicitly written as one simple formula.

In this interpretation, Urizen’s approach would be:

> “Give me an exact formula that tells me where to go at every step.”

Blake’s idea of Imagination would be closer to:

> “Look at the whole map. The meaning may not lie in a local rule for each step, but in the larger form created by many steps together.”

## The Labyrinth May Not Be Solvable by One Formula

The labyrinth may have been designed so that it cannot be solved from beginning to end with a single formula. There are rules — Möbius, totients, coordinates, mirror nodes, and so on — but these may function more like the language of the space than a complete algorithm.

For example:

> **formula → 5 possible continuations**

The formula itself does not tell you which one is correct. But when you look at the geometry:

> **branch A** breaks the previous structure  
> **branch B** leads nowhere  
> **branch C** reproduces the same kind of mirror relationship seen before

then the structure itself becomes the criterion for choosing the path.

This fits very well with the idea of going beyond form: not abandoning mathematics, but refusing to treat any single mathematical rule as the whole system.

And this creates an even more interesting paradox. You are solving a mathematical labyrinth through:

> **Reason + Imagination**

Reason gives you totients, coordinates, values, symmetries, and calculations.

Imagination gives you the ability to see what all of those elements form when they are viewed together.

---

## A Possible Meta-Meaning of Liber Primus

For Blake, the real problem is the imbalance between Reason and Imagination. Urizen turns Reason into an absolute, and as a result becomes trapped inside the very system he created.

So the possible meta-meaning of LP could be something like this:

> **You cannot escape the labyrinth simply by following the rules. First, you have to understand the rules — and then see the larger structure they create.**

That is very close to the way I have been approaching the continuation. I am not simply calculating the next symbol; I am looking at which branch causes the system to return to its own underlying structure.
